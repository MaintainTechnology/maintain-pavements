## Page 2: 01 The Problem ("The Maintenance Cycle")

![[PDF-page2.png|420]]

**Headline:** *The yard never actually gets finished.*

> Form a yard up and it starts going backwards the same month. Heavy plant concentrates load onto small footprints in the same places, water gets into the base, and what was a level pad becomes ruts, potholes and loose stone. So the grader comes back. And comes back again.

### The cycle you keep paying for
1. **Potholes and ravelling**: they start small, take water, then open up fast under traffic.
2. **Rutting and pumping** where plant stands and turns, so the same areas need making good again and again.
3. **Recurring grader and patching hours** that never actually finish the job.
4. **Loose stone** chewing tyres, and **out-of-level ground** where plant has to stand.
5. **Mud in the wet**: the yard slows down or stops, and the mud gets tracked onto the public road at the gate.
6. **Dust in the dry**, across an area where people are working on the ground.
7. **Downtime** every time an area has to come out of service to be repaired.

### Why concrete stalls
1. **Capital cost.** Engineered concrete hardstand carries a rate that's hard to justify across a whole yard.
2. **The yard stops.** Formwork, pour and strength gain take the area out of service for weeks.
3. **It is fixed.** Change the yard layout and the slab is in the wrong place.
4. **Point loads still crack it.** Repair then means saw-cutting, breaking out and disposal.
5. **Minimum quantities** make treating one or two areas uneconomic.

**Photo:** *"An untreated yard — the starting point for most sites."* An orange roller at the left edge, bare brown dirt and gravel, a black shed, trees and a ute ([[PDF-p2_img1.jpeg]]).

**Call-out panel:** *The choice is usually "keep making it good" or "pour a slab". **There is a third option.*** SuperBase builds a bound hardstand out of the base already in your yard, area by area, without a pour and without the patching cycle that follows. **60–70% below engineered concrete.**

## Page 3: 02 Method (1 of 2) ("How It Is Built")

![[PDF-page3.png|420]]

**Headline:** *The base you already have, turned into standing ground.*

> SuperBase is not a pavement, and its primary mode is not a surface spray. It is an additive introduced into the base **during the lay phase** and carried through every moisture process, so the binder sits through the full depth of the layer rather than on top of it.

| # | Step | What happens (per Jon) | Photo caption |
|---|---|---|---|
| 01 | **Prepare** | Loose stone and bull dust are graded off, and the existing yard base is **scarified to ~75 mm**. Extra roadbase is imported only where design levels need it; usually the material already in the yard is all that's needed. | *01 — Trimming the scarified base* (a CAT-badged compact track loader carrying a slatted steel frame on its bucket, under a steel shed roof, with a worker in hi-vis. It's a frame from [[V01 - Prepare]] at about 2.5–3.0 s, and the footage doesn't show an actual trimming cut; [[PDF-p3_img1.jpeg]]) |
| 02 | **Dose and mix** | SuperBase goes in with water at a **binder-to-water ratio set against your own base material**, and is mixed through the full depth of the layer. Where new base has been added, the binder runs through all moisture processes so the new layer doesn't delaminate from the old. | *02 — Binder and water through the full depth* (articulated water cart spraying under the shed; [[PDF-p3_img2.jpeg]]) |
| 03 | **Grade to level** | The treated base is trimmed to design level and crossfall while it's still workable. Getting the fall right here is what stops water ponding later, and ponded water is where potholes start. | *03 — Trimmed to level and crossfall* (CAT smooth-drum roller, unit **5335**, on a grey pad with a line of red-flag pegs; [[PDF-p3_img3.jpeg]]) |

## Page 4: 02 Method (2 of 2) ("Compaction and Proof")

![[PDF-page4.png|420]]

**Headline:** *Compaction builds the bond. Testing proves it.*

| # | Step | What happens (per Jon) | Photo caption |
|---|---|---|---|
| 04 | **Roll and compact** | **Wet rolling** to final compaction with a **smooth drum roller**. This is the stage that develops the bond: *the surface is built, not coated*, and it's where the surface strength comes from. Compaction is confirmed on site against the **density nominated in the dosage design**. | *04 — Wet rolling to final compaction* (yellow roller under the shed with a worker in orange hi-vis; [[PDF-p4_img1.jpeg]]) |
| 05 | **Cure and return to service** | The surface cures to a bound, hard-wearing hardstand and is **trafficable within hours of the final roll**, against the *weeks* a concrete pad needs to reach working strength. High-wear running lines can take an **organic top coat**, a sprayed dressing over the compacted base. | *05 — Cured and back in service* (worker in an orange hi-vis hoodie with a **backpack blower**, clearing a finished dark-grey bound surface next to garden landscaping with palms, pallets of pavers and besser blocks; [[PDF-p4_img2.jpeg]]) |

### The strength gain is measured, not asserted.
> The trial area is tested by **laboratory CBR on your own yard material, before treatment and after cure**. The result is issued with the trial report, so how much stronger the base has become is a documented figure rather than a brochure claim.

**What the trial report gives you**
- **Soaked CBR:** before treatment and after cure, on your own yard material. It's tested *soaked* because that's the condition a yard fails in.
- **Depth & compaction:** the treated depth and the density achieved across the trial area, measured on site.
- **Dosage rate:** the binder rate that produced the result, so roll-out is priced and specified off a known number.

## Page 5: 03 Applications ("Across the Yard")

![[PDF-page5.png|420]]

**Headline:** *One product, dosed to suit what each area carries.*
> The same binder is applied at different rates depending on the duty of the area: a full mixed-through treatment where heavy plant stands, a lighter dosage where trailers marshal and pass.

| Yard area | How it is applied | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| **Heavy plant standing areas** | Re-levelled and scarified through the existing base; binder and water applied across all processes through to final wet roll. | A hard, level standing area that holds its shape under concentrated loads. |
| **Stockpile & bulk material aprons** | Full mixed-through treatment, dosed for repeated loader passes and sustained stockpile loading. | Aprons that hold their shape under loaders instead of pushing out and rutting. |
| **Product & equipment laydown** | Full mixed-through treatment to carry concentrated static loads over long periods. | Stable standing for stacked product and stored plant without bedding in. |
| **Internal access & turning lanes** | Initial dosage mixed through the base, with an **organic top coat** over high-wear running lines. | Running surface that holds level under multi-axle turning instead of corrugating. |
| **Trailer marshalling & load-out** | As above, at a **lighter dosage** rate. | Marshalling area that stays trafficable through wet weather. |
| **Gate approaches & perimeter** | Lighter dosage applied **by water cart as part of routine yard watering**. | Less dust in the dry and less mud tracked onto the public road. |

**Photo strip:** *Heavy standing and turning* (a white truck with a teal tank spraying from the rear across windrowed base, a CAT grader on the left, and a dome shelter behind under clear sky; [[PDF-p5_img1.jpeg]]) · *Finished bound surface* (close-up of a light-grey bound surface with a tyre-tread mark; [[PDF-p5_img2.jpeg]]) · *Access and turning lanes* (one CAT motor grader and one smooth-drum roller parked on a wide pad in front of two white dome shelters built on blue shipping containers, overcast; [[PDF-p5_img3.jpeg]]).

## Page 6: 04 Proof & Delivery ("Evidence, and How We Prove It Here")

![[PDF-page6.png|420]]

**Headline:** *Proven as hardstand. Tested on your yard.*
> The binder technology behind SuperBase has been in **commercial use since 2017**. At the **Eloise Copper Project** it was applied across a whole site, including the **hardstand for trucks and forklifts**: concentrated wheel loads, constant turning and heavy standing, which is the same duty a plant yard asks of its surface. Your base material and traffic are different again, so the engagement starts with a trial area and CBR testing rather than a claim.

### What Maintain Pavements provides
- **Supply and logistics** to your yard.
- **Dosage design** for your actual base material, not a generic rate card.
- **CBR testing** before and after, issued as a trial report.
- **Application methodology** and method statements for approval.
- **On-site supervision** for the first application.
- **Crew training** so your own operators run it from then on.

### How we start
1. **Yard walk.** Review the areas, the base material and what the current surface costs to hold each year.
2. **Trial area.** Nominate one representative area and treat it.
3. **Test and report.** CBR before and after, with dosage and depth confirmed.
4. **Roll out by area.** Staged so the yard keeps trading.
5. **Handover.** Your crew and your plant, running it as routine work.

**CTA panel:** ***Nominate one area. We will treat it, test it, and put the numbers beside a concrete quote and a year of patching.*** A trial area tests the surface, the strength figure and the business case at the same time, on your material, in your yard.

### Disclaimer (verbatim; this governs every claim we put in the video)
> Figures shown are indicative only and are not an offer or a guarantee of performance or saving. Unit rates, dosage, depth, cure times, CBR results and savings vary with base material, climate and surface duty, and are confirmed by site trial and material testing before any commitment. Comparative pricing is against engineered concrete hardstand construction. SuperBase is not a substitute for engineered ground assessment, and where plant imposes critical loads those remain to be assessed against tested figures for the specific area.
